# Brackenwake: the story

This is the book the zones are written from. Everything in it fits the world
that already exists: the twenty one zones of `zones.js` and their one-line
moods, the four bosses already in `monsters.js`, the ore ladder, the mines, the
ruins and the words on them. Nothing here asks the map to move. It asks the map
to mean something.

The user's brief: who are we, what are we doing here, what is the mission; a
storyline per zone, each connected to the next, with a progression; friends,
enemies, a plot that thickens; dungeons, a boss and semi-bosses per zone, open
world encounters; and a cinematic entry for every zone, whose story the zone
then tells as you walk it.

One pillar in `00-OVERVIEW.md` has to be read carefully against this: "The
world is the content. There are no quests." That pillar stands. What it rules
out is a man with an exclamation mark handing you a list. What it does not rule
out is a world that knows what you have seen and speaks to it. Section 8 says
how the story is delivered without a single quest log entry.

---

## 1. The pitch, in one breath

Three hundred years ago something fell out of the sky into the south of a
kingdom that held its land by holding its water. The sea came up, the kilns went
cold, the king welcomed the thing that fell and burned his hall to glass around
it, and the kingdom drowned or scattered. What it left behind is called the
Wake: a slow tide of wrongness that has crept inward from the coasts for three
centuries and stopped, every time, at the hedges of one green valley in the
middle of the continent.

You live in that valley. This spring the hedges have started to fail. Wolves in
the meadow at night. Dead men walking out of the barrows. An old woman who
keeps the boundary stones hands you a cloak and says the thing the valley has
said to one person in every generation: **walk out and find out why.**

You are not the chosen one. You are the one who was sent.

## 2. The setting

### The name

*Bracken* is the fern that grows over everything nobody tends. *Wake* is two
things at once: the water a ship leaves behind it, and the night the living sit
up with the dead. Brackenwake is a continent sitting up with a dead kingdom,
and the ferns are winning.

### The Kingdom of the Weirs

The old kingdom was never named for a king. It was named for what it built. The
continent is wet: a slow river through the heart, a shallow sound to the east,
fens to the south east, moor and peat to the north. The first people who held it
did so with weirs, mills and sea gates, and their rulers were called the Weir
Kings because that was the whole of their claim: *we hold the water, so the
land is ours.*

Their works are still on the map. Weircross and the Weir Steps and the Weir
Tower. The Drowned Mill whose river moved. The Hollow Workings. Every ruin with
"weir" in its name is a piece of the thing that used to keep the sea and the
fens in their places.

They built seven great weirs. This is the fact the whole story turns on, and
the player learns it in the second act. Six were made of stone and water. The
seventh was made of something else, and it is under the Bracken Vale.

### The Fall

Three hundred years ago, in the reign of Ossian, the last Weir King, a light
came down in the south and landed in the country that is now the Sallow Wastes.
"Nothing grows and something fell here, and the second fact explains the
first." The thing that fell is never called a star in this story, because the
people who saw it did not think it was one. Ossian rode south to see it and
came back changed, and he called it **the Guest**, because he had decided to
welcome it.

He had it brought north and east to his hall on the coast, and there he tried
to make the kingdom's seventh weir out of it: a weir against death itself. The
hall burned so hot the coast turned to black glass, which is Cinderreach now,
and Ossian is still on his chair in the middle of it. "He is still on the
chair. That is the problem."

The sea came up the same year. Whether the Guest raised it or the weirs simply
failed with no one left to keep them is a question the story leaves to the
player, because the people in the story disagree about it. Half the eastern
kingdom went under. Its knight marshal, Sir Caradoc, held the sea gate at the
Drowned Steps until the water was over his head and is still holding it: "He
walks up them every night and finds the sea still there."

### The Wake

What the Guest left behind is not an army and not a curse. It is a tide. It
comes in from the rim of the world a little further every generation: blossom
out of season in Witchwood with no birds in it, ice a hand thick in the Cold
Adit when it is not winter, heather that burned three hundred years ago and
never grew back. Where it is thick, dead things do not stay down, and living
things grow into something else. Where it is thin, the country is just a little
wrong, and the people living in it have learned not to look at the wrong part.

This is why the danger of the world rises with distance from the heart. The map
already does this. The story says why.

### The Vale

The Bracken Vale is the one place the Wake has never entered. Hedged fields, a
slow river, nobody in a hurry. The people of the Vale do not talk about why
they are safe, in the way people do not talk about a thing they are afraid of
finding out. They keep the hedges. They keep the boundary stones. They send out
a Walker when the stones say to.

The reason they are safe is under their feet. The Bracken Sink, the dungeon in
the heart of the Vale, is the seventh weir, and it was not built by Ossian. It
was built by the people who fled him, out of the only material that had ever
held the Wake back: someone willing to stand in it. The Sink has always had a
keeper. The last one died the winter before the game begins, and the hedges
have been failing since.

## 3. Who you are

You are a Walker.

In the Vale, the hedge is a thing you walk. Every family takes a turn: a season
walking the boundary, mending gaps, resetting stones, noting what has come
through in the night. Most Walkers never leave the Vale. But the stones are
older than the hedge, and when they are read a certain way, they name someone,
and that person walks out.

You are that person this generation. The ten openings in creation are what you
were before the stones named you: a soldier back from somewhere, a ranger who
knew the Thornwood edge, a healer, a thief, a smith. The Vale does not care.
Maud Fallow, who reads the stones, does not care either. "The stones said a
name. I do not have to like it."

The player writes their own name in creation and that is the name the stones
said. Every named character in the story uses it.

**What you are not.** You are not of royal blood. You are not the reincarnated
king. The world does not end if you fail. The Wake would take the Vale in a
generation or two and the people would scatter as everyone else did, and the
continent would go on being green and wrong with nobody in it. The stakes are a
valley, and the people in it, and that is enough.

**The one thing that is special about you.** You can stand in the Wake longer
than other people. Not for ever. This is what the stones read for, and it is
what a keeper of the Sink has to be able to do. The player learns this about
themselves slowly, from what other people say, and the ending turns on what it
means.

## 4. The people

Six people carry the story across the whole map. Each one is met early, changes
across the acts, and is standing somewhere specific at the end.

**Maud Fallow.** Hedge-reeve of the Vale. Seventy, sharp, reads the boundary
stones and has for fifty years. She sends you. She knows more than she says
about the Sink, because her sister was its keeper and died last winter, and she
has not decided whether to tell you what a keeper is. Her arc is deciding. She
never leaves the Vale; she is the voice the story comes home to. Her last scene
is at the mouth of the Sink.

**Tam Weir.** Fourteen, the miller's boy in the Mill Run, the first friend the
player makes outside the Vale. He attaches himself and will not be sent home.
He is comic relief for one act and then he is not: in the second act the Weir
Tower's stones name his family, and he is the last of the line that built the
weirs, which is why the Sink calls to him and why Hesk wants him. He grows up
across the game. He is seventeen by the end and he is the one who offers to
take the keeper's place, and the player's last decision is whether to let him.

**Brother Hesk.** A scholar of the Grey Hall on Ashen Moor, the only man on the
continent who can still read the old script. Kind, patient, genuinely curious,
and the story's betrayer, though he never thinks of it that way. He believes the
Wake is a misunderstanding: that the Guest is not malign, only asleep and
dreaming badly, and that if it were woken and spoken to properly the Wake would
stop. He is possibly right. He deciphers the Guest's writing at the end of the
second act and goes south to wake it, taking what he needs from the player's
finds, and by the third act the Sallow Wastes have a new lich in them who still
speaks in Hesk's voice and still wants to explain.

**Ysolt of the Strand.** Salvager, boat owner, the only person who will take you
across water. Forties, sardonic, has lost two boats and a brother to the Drowned
Coast. Runs the Broken Strand's camp. She is the story's pragmatist: she does
not care why the Wake exists, she wants the sea to give her brother back. Her
arc runs through the Drowned Knight: he has her brother's body among the
drowned who walk up the steps every night, and what she does about that is the
Drowned Coast's ending.

**Fern.** A girl of about ten who keeps the moss off a stone in Witchwood, and
who nobody has ever seen doing it. She is dead, and has been for three hundred
years, and she is a Weir: Ossian's youngest, who was sent inland when the sea
came and did not survive the journey. She does not know she is dead. She is the
gentlest thing in the game and the key to the middle act, because she remembers
the seven weirs as a nursery rhyme. She cannot leave her stone. At the end,
someone has to tell her.

**The Ashen King.** Ossian. Not a villain who wants anything. He is the man who
made the worst decision in the history of the continent for a reason he still
believes was love, and he has been sitting with it for three centuries. He can
be fought. He would rather be asked a question. He is the story's mirror for
the player: he stood in the Wake longer than anyone, and this is what it did.

And the enemy, which is not a person. **The Guest** never speaks and is never
seen whole. It is the crater, the ore that burns, the web that is load bearing,
the cold that keeps a hill from thawing, the dark trees that keep step. The
four Powers of the rim are each a piece of the world that grew up around it,
and Hesk is what happens when a man decides to introduce himself.

## 5. The shape of the journey

Four acts. The first three follow the three rings the zones already sit in,
which means the danger bands the world already uses are the story's pacing.
The fourth is one dungeon, at home.

| act | ring | tiers | what the player is doing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I. The Hedge | the Vale and the six zones at 3 km | 1 to 2 | finding out what has come through, and that it came from further out |
| II. The Kingdom | the eight zones at 4.4 km | 3 to 4 | finding out what the kingdom was, what a weir is, and what is under the Vale |
| III. The Vigils | the six zones at 6.6 km | 4 to 5 | facing the four Powers, losing Hesk, reaching the Fall |
| IV. The Wake | the Bracken Sink | 5 | the seventh weir, and who keeps it |

The acts are not gates. A player can walk to Cinderreach on the first day and
die there, and the world lets them. What the acts control is what the world
*says*: the people in the outer ring will talk to a stranger who has not been to
the Weir Tower, but they will not tell them anything, because the stranger does
not know the right questions. Knowledge is the progression. Section 8.

### The through-line, act by act

**Act I.** Maud sends you to the six neighbours of the Vale with one question:
what has come through, and where from. Each answers with one fact and one
friend or enemy. By the end of the act you have Tam, you have met Ysolt once,
you have a name for the thing (the Wake) from the Grey Barrows' dead, and you
know it came from the sea and the south and the north all at once, which means
it did not come from anywhere. It came from everywhere but here. Why here?

**Act II.** The question drives you into the old kingdom's ruins. Brother Hesk
at the Grey Hall reads you the first of the weir-stones. The Weir Tower's
records name seven weirs and place six. Fern in Witchwood sings the rhyme that
places the seventh: "and one where the bracken grows." The Warden of the Cut in
the Stone Garden guards the kings' tally, which says what the seventh was made
of. By the end of the act, the player knows the Vale is safe because someone is
standing under it, that the someone died last winter, and that Hesk has read
something on the Sallow tally that he will not repeat. He leaves for the south
that night. Tam, told what his name means, goes very quiet.

**Act III.** Four Powers stand between the player and the Fall, one at each
compass point of the rim, and each holds a piece of the answer to the only
question left: can the Wake be ended, or only held? The Ashen King says it
cannot be ended and he has proof, and the proof is him. The Drowned Knight says
duty is the only weir that holds and he has proof, and the proof is the sea. The
Frost thanes of the Frostcrown say the Guest sleeps because the cold makes it
sleep, and they have been paying for that cold for three hundred years. The
Mother of Spiders says nothing, because she is a spider, but her web is the
only thing on the continent the Wake has never crossed, and it is anchored in
the Teeth for a reason. The Night March is the Guest dreaming: its army keeps
step and walks up out of the Night Shaft every dusk in order, going nowhere.
And in the Sallow Wastes, at the bottom of the Fall, Hesk has done what he went
to do, and the Guest has half woken, and the Wake is moving faster than it has
in three hundred years.

**Act IV.** You come home. The hedges are gone. The Vale is fighting in its
lanes. Maud is at the mouth of the Sink and finally says what a keeper is:
someone who stands in the Wake so that other people do not have to, for as long
as they can, and then someone else. Her sister did it for forty years. The
stones named you because you could. Tam says he is a Weir and it should be
him. The Sink is a dungeon with the Wake pouring up it, and at the bottom is the
place where the last keeper sat, and the game's last choice is who sits down.

There are three endings, and the systems already exist to carry them: the
character record, and a world that can be told to change.

- **You sit.** The Wake recedes to where it was. The Vale is safe for a
  lifetime, yours. The credits play over the Vale in summer and the character
  is retired to the keeper's chair, in the game, for ever: a new character can
  walk down and find them there. This is the "true" ending only in the sense
  that it costs the most.
- **Tam sits.** He is a Weir and he is stronger in it than you would have been.
  The Vale is safe for his lifetime, which is long. You walk out of the Sink
  into the Vale and keep playing, and you can visit him, and he gets older,
  and he does not blame you. Maud does not speak to you again.
- **Nobody sits.** You take Hesk's side after all, go back to the Fall and
  finish what he started: wake the Guest fully and speak to it. This is the
  hardest fight in the game and it is a dialogue as much as a fight. The Wake
  stops. Everywhere. The rim thaws, the blossom in Witchwood falls, the Drowned
  Knight walks up the steps and finds the sea gone down. And the Guest is
  awake, and it is still here, and the last shot of the game is the crater with
  something standing in it, looking north.

## 6. The zones

Every zone below is written to the same frame, so the same author can write
them and the same systems can carry them:

- **Arrival.** What the entry cinematic shows, in one paragraph. This is the
  brief for the video the user will make. It is also the toast under the banner
  the first time you walk in.
- **The story here.** What this zone is about and how it connects.
- **People.** Who is standing here and what they want.
- **The threads.** The beats the zone tells as you walk it, in the order a
  player would most likely meet them. Not quests. Things that are true in the
  world and get said when you have earned them.
- **Underground.** The dungeon or cave, what is at the bottom, and its boss or
  keeper.
- **Out in the open.** Named encounters: the semi-bosses. Each is one monster
  from the roster, promoted with a name, a place, a habit and something in its
  pockets.
- **What carries forward.** The one fact or object that the next zone wants.

Danger tiers are the zone's own. Bosses are placed where `monsters.js` already
puts them; every other named encounter is a promotion of a rostered monster,
which `monster_models.js` can already draw.

### Act I: The Hedge

#### The Bracken Vale
*Hedged fields, a slow river, and nobody in a hurry. Whatever the rest of the
world turns out to be, it starts here.* Tier 1. Home.

**Arrival.** Dawn over hedged fields. A woman in a grey shawl walking a
boundary of standing stones, touching each as she passes. She stops at one and
reads it for a long time. Cut to a wolf's print in a lane, full of dew. Cut to
the same woman at your door, holding out a cloak.

**The story here.** The Vale is safety, and the story's job in the Vale is to
make the player love it before it is threatened. The first hour is farms, a
river, a town, people who know your name. The first night is the first wolf,
and the first skeleton walking up out of the churchyard, and the meadow spawn
tables already do exactly this: rats and boar and goblins by day, wolves and
dead by night. The Vale is the one zone where the story never leaves: every act
returns here, and its state changes with each. In Act I it is worried. In Act
II it is arming. In Act III the hedges are down and there are barricades in the
lanes. In Act IV it is a battlefield.

**People.** Maud Fallow, hedge-reeve. The Sink's mouth is behind her cottage
and she never mentions it. A blacksmith who taught you, a healer who bound your
first wound, an innkeeper who has heard every Walker's story and tells you what
the last one said before he left: "he said he would be back by harvest."

**The threads.**
1. Maud reads the stones and names you. The town knows by noon.
2. The first night. Something comes through the hedge. What it is depends on
   where you are standing; the world decides.
3. Maud shows you the gap. Wolf prints going in, going out. She says the six
   words: *walk out and find out why.*
4. The innkeeper gives you the last Walker's cloak. It has a mill-wheel sewn on
   the inside, badly, by a child. (Tam's. His father was the last Walker.)
5. Each return through the acts: the Vale has changed, and Maud has one more
   thing to say that she should have said before.

**Underground.** The Bracken Sink. Sealed in Acts I to III: the door behind
Maud's cottage is a door and it does not open, and she says "not yet" and
nothing else. It opens in Act IV and it is the last dungeon in the game.

**Out in the open.** *Old Grist*, a boar the size of a pony that has killed
three dogs, in the beech hangar above the river. *The Churchyard Sexton*, a
skeleton in a torn surplice that walks the graves at night and rings the bell
if it sees you. Neither is the Wake. Both are what the Vale has always had and
lived with, and that is the point: the Vale was never soft.

**What carries forward.** The cloak, and Maud's question.

#### The Mill Run
*Wheat, a water mill, and the first hole in the ground anyone will let you
into.* Tier 1 to 2. North.

**Arrival.** A mill wheel turning in a fast stream, wheat to the horizon, a boy
on the roof of the mill with a slingshot watching the road. The camera follows
his gaze to the green hill behind the mill, and three black mouths in it, and a
yard trodden flat.

**The story here.** The Mill Run is where the player learns the world has a
past. The Millrun Adit is a copper and tin mine that has been worked for a
century by people who do not ask what the older marks on the walls are. The
miller's boy, Tam, does ask. He has been down all three cuts and he has a map
and he wants to show someone. The Mill Run is also where the story makes its
first friend, and the first person the player will lose if they are careless:
Tam follows you. In Act I he is a voice at your elbow and a slingshot in the
Adit. He goes home when told and comes back the next day.

**People.** Tam Weir. His mother, who runs the mill alone and does not want him
going out and knows she cannot stop him. A foreman at the Adit who will sell
you a pickaxe and knows the third cut has "a wall that is not ours."

**The threads.**
1. Tam finds you on the road. He knows who you are; the Vale's news reaches the
   mill by the miller's cart.
2. The Adit: three cuts of copper and tin, and in the third, behind a fall of
   rock the miners never cleared, a wall of fitted stone with the weir-mark on
   it. The first weir. It is broken.
3. Tam's mother tells you what happened to his father: the last Walker, gone
   north to Ashen Moor and not back by harvest, or the harvest after.
4. Leaving, Tam is on the road ahead of you with a bag.

**Underground.** The Millrun Adit, three cuts. Copper, tin, iron. Rats, bats,
thorn grubs, and in the third cut, a *Goblin Scout* band that has been living
in the old weir-chamber and wearing its stones as armour. Their chief is the
cut's keeper: *Sallow Pike*, a goblin warrior in a breastplate made of a
weir-stone, who has scratched a copy of the mark onto his own face.

**Out in the open.** *The Miller's Son*, a bandit captain who used to be exactly
that at a mill up the Run that burned, and who holds the ford at night with
six men. He is not a monster; he can be talked past with the mill's name.

**What carries forward.** Tam. And the weir-mark, seen once, not yet understood.

#### Saltmere
*A shallow sound that is half land at low tide and none of it at high.* Tier 2.
North east.

**Arrival.** A tide going out over a mile of sand, fast, and stone steps coming
up out of the water as it goes: the Weir Steps, going down into a floor that
is now the sea bed. Crabs the size of dogs coming out of the weed. A boat with
one sail, beating in against the wind, a woman at the tiller not looking at
the steps at all.

**The story here.** Saltmere is the first time the player sees what the sea
did. The Weir Steps are the top of a drowned building, and the tide reveals it
twice a day and takes it back. It is also where Ysolt is first met, briefly:
she salvages the Steps at low water and will not talk about why the water is
where it is. Saltmere is the sea's threat made small and legible before the
Drowned Coast makes it enormous.

**People.** Ysolt of the Strand, passing through, salvaging. A hamlet of
cocklers who have a rhyme about the Steps that ends "and the king came up to
count them." A drowned man who stands on the Steps at high water and is gone at
low.

**The threads.**
1. The tide and the Steps: walk down them at low water and there is a door at
   the bottom with the weir-mark on it. Second weir. Broken.
2. Ysolt. She will sell you what she has salvaged and say one true thing: "the
   water is not rising. It is looking for something."
3. The cocklers' rhyme, which is the first mention of a king.
4. At high water, the drowned man. He is *Saltmere's Reeve*, the local named
   encounter, and he was the Steps' last keeper. He does not attack until you
   go down.

**Underground.** None authored. The Weir Steps are a ruin, not a dungeon: the
door at the bottom does not open, and will not until Act III's Drowned Coast.

**Out in the open.** *The Tide-Widow*, a Drowned in a bridal shift who walks the
sand at the turn of every tide, exactly where the water's edge will be in an
hour, so that anyone who follows her is caught by it. *The Reeve*, above.

**What carries forward.** Ysolt's name, and the fact that the Steps have a door.

#### The Sedge Flats
*Standing water to the knee and sedge to the shoulder, and something in it that
is not a frog.* Tier 1 to 2. South east.

**Arrival.** Sedge moving in a wind that is not blowing anything else. A mill
wheel standing in dry grass with no river near it. The camera drops to water
level and something long moves under the surface toward it.

**The story here.** The Sedge Flats are the fens the weirs used to hold back,
and the Drowned Mill is the proof: a mill whose river moved when the weir at
the Hollow Hills failed, so that the wheel stands in a field and the flood
stands where the field was. The Flats teach the player that the Wake does not
only bring monsters: it moves the world. Bog crawlers and ghouls live in the
water. The people here are eel-fishers who have gone strange, not from the
Wake but from being the only ones who stayed.

**People.** A fen-family at the Drowned Mill who have made the millhouse a
stilt-house and will trade eels for anything metal. Their grandmother remembers
the river moving "in my mother's mother's time," which puts a date on it, the
first the player gets.

**The threads.**
1. The Drowned Mill. Inside, a millstone with the weir-mark cut into it by the
   miller as a charm, the third mark. It did not work.
2. The grandmother's date. Two hundred and eighty years. Roughly.
3. The thing in the water: it is *Long Sarah*, a Bog Crawler grown beyond its
   kind, and the fen-family feed it a sheep a month so it leaves them alone.
   Killing it frees them and they are not grateful.

**Underground.** None. The Flats are open water and the fear of what is in it.

**Out in the open.** *Long Sarah*. *The Eel-Man*, a Ghoul in fisherman's oilskins
who still checks the traps every night and will follow you for a mile in the
sedge without ever quite attacking, until you stop.

**What carries forward.** A date, and the direction the river went: south, to
the Hollow Hills.

#### Kiln Heath
*Gorse, dry sand and the brick kilns that gave it the name, all of them cold.*
Tier 2. South.

**Arrival.** A row of brick kilns like beehives across a heath of yellow gorse,
every one of them cold, one of them with its door bricked up from the outside.
A wind takes ash off the heath in a long veil. Under the largest kiln, stairs.

**The story here.** Kiln Heath fired the bricks the kingdom was built from, and
the Kiln Cellars under the biggest kiln are where the bricks were stacked to
cure. Now the kilns are cold and the Cellars are full of bandits who found them
first and cultists who found the bandits. The Heath is the first place the
player meets the Wake's human face: people who went looking for the Guest on
purpose. The cultists here are the outer edge of Hesk's eventual road, though
Hesk would be appalled to hear it.

**People.** A brickmaker's widow at Fallow's Adit who mines clay and sells
pots and knows which kiln has the stair. A cultist who has come out of the
Cellars to sit in the sun and has changed his mind and will tell you the
Cellars' layout in exchange for being walked to the Vale.

**The threads.**
1. The bricked-up kiln. Somebody bricked it from outside with someone inside.
   The pot-widow did it. She does not say why until Act III.
2. Fallow's Adit: a dry cave propped with rotten timber, copper and tin, the
   widow's clay pit at the back, and a Thorn Grub nest.
3. The Kiln Cellars: bandits above, cultists below, and at the bottom the
   fourth weir-mark, cut into the kiln's foundation, with the cultists praying
   at it because they think it is the Guest's mark. It is the opposite.

**Underground.** The Kiln Cellars, two levels. Bandits and goblin warriors
above; cultists below. The keeper is *the Kiln-Warden*, a Cultist who has
burned his own face in the one kiln that still works and speaks the first words
the player hears in the Guest's language, which Hesk will later translate as
"open."

**Out in the open.** *The Bricked Man*, what came out of the bricked-up kiln
when the player opens it: a Zombie, the widow's husband, who went into the
Cellars and came back different, and she could not kill him, so she walled him
in. He is the game's first small tragedy and he should be sad to fight.

**What carries forward.** The word "open," and the fact that some people want
the Wake.

#### The Grey Barrows
*Grass mounds in rows, and the rows go on past where anyone bothered to
count.* Tier 2 to 3. South west.

**Arrival.** Grass mounds in rows under a grey sky, a thousand of them, and the
camera rising until the rows resolve into a pattern: a spiral, and at its centre
one standing stone that is not the biggest. Night falls in the shot. A mound
opens.

**The story here.** The Barrows are the kingdom's dead, and the kingdom buried
its dead in the order they died, so the spiral is a calendar. Walk it inward
and the graves get newer. The outermost ring is a thousand years old. The
innermost is three hundred, and it is where the rows stop, because after that
there was nobody left to bury anyone. This is where the player learns the word
*Wake*, from the dead themselves: a Necromancer who lives in the Long Barrow
ruin, night only, and has been asking the dead questions for thirty years.

**People.** *Aldous the Quiet*, the Necromancer of the Long Barrow. Not evil,
not kind, curious in the way Hesk is curious but without Hesk's hope. He will
teach the player the first bones of necromancy and tell them what the dead
call what killed them: they call it the Wake, and they say it came in on the
tide.

**The threads.**
1. The spiral. Walking it inward, mound by mound, is the zone's story: the
   graves get richer, then poorer, then hurried, then stop.
2. Aldous, at night, at the Long Barrow. He knows what you are; the dead told
   him. He is the one who says the Vale has a keeper, and will not say more.
3. The Barrow Cellars: the last king's family vault, "cut by people who
   expected to be visited." Nobody visited. Inside, a row of stone coffins,
   and the last one is empty and its lid is off, and the name on it is Ossian.
4. At the innermost mound, at midnight, the Barrows' dead stand up in their
   rows and face south east, toward the Fall, and stand until dawn. They do not
   attack anyone who stands with them.

**Underground.** The Barrow Cellars. Skeletons, zombies, ghouls, and a *Bone
Knight* keeper who was the royal family's tomb guard and is still guarding a
row of coffins with one empty. *Sir Anselm of the Vault* has a name because he
will tell it to you before he attacks, and he will let you leave if you do not
touch the coffins.

**Out in the open.** *The Counting Man*, a Wraith that walks the rows at night
counting under its breath and loses its place if you say a number aloud, which
enrages it. *The Rows at Midnight*, above, an event rather than a monster.

**What carries forward.** The word Wake. The name Ossian. An empty coffin.

#### Thornwood
*Spruce close enough together that the light gives up two paces in.* Tier 2.
North west.

**Arrival.** Spruce so close the camera has to push through them. Silence. A
cold fire in a clearing, ash still shaped like the logs it was. Somebody's
pack, open. The camera turns slowly to the trees, and every tree has eyes at
the same height.

**The story here.** Thornwood is wolves. It is the first zone where the player
is hunted rather than attacked: the wolves of the Thornwood run in a pack with
a name and a leader, and they take a person a season from the road. The cold
fire is the last Walker's. Tam's father made it. Thornwood is where Tam finds
out, if he is with you, and where the story decides whether he goes home.

**People.** A ranger at the wood's edge, the only one who goes in, who will
teach archery and tracking and has a map of the pack's dens. Nobody else. The
wood has no hamlets. That is the point of it.

**The threads.**
1. The cold fire. Tam's father's pack. His Walker's cloak is not in it. His
   knife is.
2. The ranger's map. Seven dens, one alpha. Clearing them is the zone.
3. The alpha: *Greymaw*, a Dire Wolf with a Walker's cloak-pin in his hide, who
   does not run. Killing him gives back the pin. It matches yours.
4. Tam, at the fire, with the knife. He does not cry. He asks if he can keep
   walking with you. This is the end of Act I, whichever zone you did last.

**Underground.** None. Thornwood's dread is above ground.

**Out in the open.** *Greymaw*, above. *The Watcher in the Spruce*, a Giant
Spider that has taken the ranger's old blind twenty feet up and drops on
anyone who camps under it.

**What carries forward.** The pin. And Tam's decision.

### Act II: The Kingdom

#### The Broken Strand
*A coast that came apart. Half of it is islands now and the other half is
deciding.* Tier 3. East.

**Arrival.** A coastline shattered into stacks and islands, sea running white
between them. A camp on the last piece of mainland, boats drawn up, a woman
mending a sail with a needle the size of a knife. Out on the furthest stack, a
cave mouth with a tide mark above head height.

**The story here.** The Strand is Ysolt's, and it is the story's harbour: from
here she will take you to the Drowned Coast in Act III, and it is here the
sea's half of the story is told plainly. Crook's Delve, the cave on the stack,
was a sea-cave the tide got into once, three hundred years ago, and the mark
above your head is how high. The Strand is where the player understands the
scale of the flood, because the ground they stand on used to be ten miles from
the sea.

**People.** Ysolt, at home, with her crew of salvagers, harpies nesting on her
stacks and crabs in her boats. She has a chart of the Drowned Coast that she
made herself over twenty years, and a name she does not say: her brother's.

**The threads.**
1. Ysolt's camp. She remembers you from Saltmere. She will teach fishing and
   sailing and sell you what the sea gave back.
2. Crook's Delve: iron and silver, and at the back, in the dark above the tide
   mark, a boat. Not a wreck: drawn up, three hundred years ago, by people
   fleeing east who found the sea had beaten them there. Their bones are in it.
   One of them wore a weir-key on a chain, the first key of the story, and it
   has the fifth mark on it.
3. Ysolt's brother. She says it once, late, at the fire. He went to the Drowned
   Steps to see if the stories were true and did not come back, and she has
   seen him since, at the top of the steps, at night, in the line.
4. Ysolt will not sail to the Drowned Coast for any money. In Act III she will
   sail for a reason.

**Underground.** Crook's Delve, a cave. Crabs, harpies, drowned. Its keeper is
*Mother Crab*, a Crab promoted to the size of a cart, who has made the boat her
den and does not want you in it.

**Out in the open.** *The Stack-Queen*, a Harpy on the tallest stack who has
learned to say three words and says them to sailors: "come up here." *Ysolt's
Bad Luck*, a Drowned in salvager's gear who follows her boats along the shore.
It is not her brother. She has checked.

**What carries forward.** The first weir-key. Ysolt, who will sail when she has
a reason.

#### The Ember Flats
*Sand, red stone and heat off the ground at noon. The ore here burns.* Tier 3
to 4. South east.

**Arrival.** Red rock and white sand under a sun that is too big. A mine yard
where the rails have warped from heat. A man breaking ore with a hammer, and
the ore glowing where it breaks, and him not looking at it. Behind him, in the
heat shimmer, a manticore lying on a rock like a cat.

**The story here.** The Ember Cut is the first emberite the player sees, and
emberite is the first ore that is *of* the Guest: it fell with it, or grew from
where it fell, and it burns without fire. The Flats are the beginning of the
south's story, the road the Guest was carried along from the Fall to Ossian's
hall, and everything along it caught. The miners here are hard people who
mine a thing they know is wrong because it is worth more than anything else on
the continent, and the cultists of Kiln Heath came from here.

**People.** The Cut's mine-captain, *Rook*, a woman with burn scars to the
elbow who will trade emberite for iron and tell you the road: "they carried it
north east, to the coast. You can see where. The sand is glass." A cultist camp
on the Flats' edge that is recruiting, openly.

**The threads.**
1. The Ember Cut: iron and emberite, four cuts, and the cuts are warm. The
   deepest has a floor of fused sand, and the shape of something pressed into
   it: a sledge, and the ruts of what dragged it.
2. The glass road: a line of fused sand running north east across the Flats
   toward Cinderreach, visible from any height. Following it is the zone.
3. The cultists. They know your name now. Their captain offers you the Guest's
   mark and says Hesk's name, which the player may or may not know yet.
4. Rook's warning, on leaving: "do not take the ore into the Vale."

**Underground.** The Ember Cut, four cuts, the deepest a small dungeon. Giant
spiders, cultists, and at the fused floor, *the Sledge-Master*, an Ogre in
cultist rags who has decided he is the Guest's bearer and hauls an empty sledge
around the chamber for ever.

**Out in the open.** *Noon*, a Manticore that hunts only in the heat of the day
and lies on the same red rock every noon like a sundial. *The Recruiter*, the
cultist captain, who can be fought or listened to.

**What carries forward.** The glass road, pointing at Cinderreach. Hesk's name
in the wrong mouth.

#### The Hollow Hills
*Green hills with the sound wrong under them, and doors in the sides.* Tier 3.
South.

**Arrival.** Green downs, a footstep echoing where there should be no echo, and
a door set into a hillside, closed, with light behind it. A tower on the
skyline, roofless, watching a road that is not there. The camera goes down
through the turf to a hall the size of a cathedral, and something moving in
it.

**The story here.** The Hollow Hills are the kingdom's granaries and the fifth
weir: the Hollow Workings were cut to hold grain for a siege that never came,
and the weir under them held the river that now runs through the Sedge Flats.
When it broke, the river moved and the Hills' people went underground to live
in the workings, and something else lived there too. The Weir Tower is where
the player finally reads the kingdom's own account: it kept the road, and it
kept the records, and the records name seven weirs and place six.

**People.** The Hills have a hamlet of people who live half underground and
speak quietly, because the sound carries. *Old Wynn*, the Weir Tower's last
warden, blind, who has memorised the records because the pages are going, and
will recite them to anyone who brings him the Barrows' name. He is the one who
first says: "seven weirs. Six of stone. And one where the bracken grows."

**The threads.**
1. The doors in the hillsides. Some open. Some are painted on.
2. The Weir Tower and Old Wynn. The recitation. The list of six places, five of
   which the player has seen a mark at. The sixth is the Stone Garden. The
   seventh he will not place: "the rhyme places it, and I was never taught the
   rhyme."
3. The Hollow Workings: a mine that became a home that became something else.
   The people who lived here left in a hurry, and left the tables set.
4. Tam, if present, recognises a name in the recitation. Weir. He asks Wynn
   what it means. Wynn asks him his name, and goes quiet, and then says: "then
   you should not go south."

**Underground.** The Hollow Workings, two levels. Ghouls above, in the homes;
below, in the granary hall, *the Granary Wraith*, the Hills' keeper, who was
the Workings' quartermaster and still counts stores that rotted three
centuries ago, and a Mire Troll that came up from the flooded weir-channel and
lives in the bottom of the hall.

**Out in the open.** *The Painted Door*, which is not a monster but an event: a
door that is painted on opens once, at night, when you are near, and a Hobgoblin
war party comes out of it and it is painted again in the morning. *The Echo*,
a Harpy that has learned the Hills' acoustics and calls in voices from a mile
off.

**What carries forward.** The list of six. The word "rhyme." Tam's name, out
loud.

#### Witchwood
*Blossom out of season and no birds in any of it.* Tier 3. South west.

**Arrival.** Cherry blossom falling in a wood where nothing else is in leaf. No
birdsong; the silence is the sound. A standing stone with the moss freshly
scraped off, and the scrapings still wet, and nobody there. The camera holds on
the stone until a small hand comes into frame and brushes a petal off it.

**The story here.** Witchwood is the Wake's gentlest and strangest face. The
blossom is the wood trying to be spring for someone. Fern is the someone. She
keeps her stone and sings her rhyme and does not know she is dead, and the
wood has grown up around her grief for three hundred years into a place where
nothing is quite right and nothing wants to hurt her. The player comes for the
rhyme, and the rhyme places the seventh weir, and getting it means sitting with
a dead child and not telling her.

**People.** Fern. Nobody else lives here. Her stone is the shrine, "Fern's
Stone," and she will only appear to someone who is alone and has not drawn a
weapon in the wood.

**The threads.**
1. The wood. Blossom, silence, spiders in the blossom, and the feeling of being
   watched by something that means no harm.
2. The stone. Wait. Fern comes. She thinks you are a servant sent to fetch her
   home to the hall by the sea, and she asks if her father has stopped being
   angry.
3. The rhyme. She sings it if asked nicely, and it is the seven weirs as a
   skipping song, and it ends: "and one where the bracken grows, and one to
   keep it, and nobody knows." She does not know what it means. She learned it
   in the nursery.
4. She asks you to tell her father she is being good. The player can promise.
   The promise is remembered, and kept or broken in Cinderreach.

**Underground.** None. Witchwood does not go down.

**Out in the open.** *The Blossom Mother*, a Giant Spider that has made the
whole canopy one web so fine the blossom hangs in it, and which drops
everything on anyone who draws steel near the stone. *The Birds*, an event: at
dusk, once, every bird that should be in the wood arrives at once and sits in
the trees in silence for a minute and leaves.

**What carries forward.** The rhyme. The promise.

#### The Iron Shoulder
*Bare stone shoulders with the ore showing on the outside of them, which is why
anybody came.* Tier 3 to 4. West.

**Arrival.** Mountains with the ore showing in blue veins across bare rock. Two
mine yards on one hillside, one worn to rock, the older one colder. An iron
golem standing perfectly still at the old cut's mouth with snow on its
shoulders, which means it has stood there since before the snow.

**The story here.** The Shoulder is the kingdom's arsenal and the story's
forge. The Deep Shoulder and the Low Shoulder are iron, silver and coldiron,
and coldiron is the second ore of the Guest: not fallen, but grown where the
Wake's cold pooled, and it takes an edge nothing else does. The golems here
were the kingdom's, made to guard the mines, and they are still guarding them
from the only thing left to guard them from, which is you. The Shoulder is
where the player arms for Act III, and where the smiths who can work the
Guest's metals still live.

**People.** A mining town in the Shoulder's lee, the hardest town in the game,
with the only smith who works coldiron, *Hild Ironshoulder*, who will teach it
for a price and asks where you mean to use it. A golem at the Low Shoulder's
mouth that does not attack and says one word if you stand in front of it for a
minute: "pass."

**The threads.**
1. The two mines. The Deep Shoulder is worked and hostile: ogres have taken
   the fourth mouth. The Low Shoulder is old and cold and its golem lets you
   pass and nobody knows why.
2. In the Low Shoulder's deepest cut, the kingdom's armoury: racks, rusted
   empty, and one coldiron blade still in its stand with the weir-mark on the
   hilt. The sixth key. It is a sword.
3. Hild, shown the sword, goes quiet, then tells you what coldiron is and what
   it does to the man who carries it long enough. Then she sharpens it.
4. The golems, if you carry the sword, all turn to face you as you pass. None
   attack.

**Underground.** The Deep Shoulder, four cuts. Ogres and an Iron Golem keeper
in the fourth, *the Foreman*, who is the kingdom's last working golem and is
still running the shift. The Low Shoulder, two cuts, empty but for cold and one
Wyvern that has denned in the second and is the reason the golem says pass: it
wants you to deal with it.

**Out in the open.** *The Stone Shepherd*, an Ogre that keeps a flock of
stoneback bears on the high shoulder and calls them down on climbers. *The
Watch at the Low Shoulder*, the speaking golem, which can be fought if you
insist and is very sorry.

**What carries forward.** The coldiron sword: the sixth key, and a weapon.

#### The Long Dark
*Old forest, and old means the trees were here before the word for them.* Tier
3 to 4. North west.

**Arrival.** Trees so old their trunks are rooms. No canopy visible; the light
is green and comes from nowhere. Werewolf tracks in moss that has not been
stepped on in a century. Deep in, a wall of living wood with a face in it, eyes
closed. It opens them.

**The story here.** The Long Dark is the oldest thing on the continent and the
only one that remembers before the kingdom. The Elder Treants here were the
kingdom's western border and its treaty: the Weir Kings did not cut the Long
Dark and the Long Dark did not walk. The Wake has been in the wood for three
centuries and the wood has been holding it, alone, the way the Vale has, by
standing in it. The eldest treant is the story's one witness who was there,
and it will speak if brought the right thing, and what it says reframes the
Guest: "it did not fall. It was dropped."

**People.** No people. Werewolves, who were people, and the treants, who were
never people and are the closest thing the wood has to them. *Marl*, a
prospector's ghost at Marl's Seam, who found the verdite and died of it and
will show you where.

**The threads.**
1. The wood. Getting lost is the story: the Long Dark has no roads and the
   trees move at night, slightly.
2. Marl's Seam: a cave with verdite in the wall, "green gold in the wall of it,
   if the light is right and you are not." Verdite is the third ore of the
   Guest and it is the one that grew where the Wake touched living things.
3. The Elder, in the heart of the wood, a treant older than the others by a
   thousand years. It speaks to someone carrying the coldiron sword, because
   the sword was forged from the wood's own cold. It says the Guest was dropped
   and asks nothing.
4. The werewolves. They are the wood's own keepers, changed by three centuries
   of standing in the Wake with it, and the pack leader was a Weir knight.

**Underground.** Marl's Seam, a cave. Giant spiders, thorn grubs, and *Marl
Himself*, a Ghoul in a prospector's coat who dug too deep into the green and
will not stop showing you the seam.

**Out in the open.** *The Elder*, above, who is not fought unless you cut a
tree in its sight. *Sir Bryn of the Wood*, a Werewolf who was a knight and
wears what is left of his surcoat, and who will duel the player once, at the
full moon, and yield at half health and say the Guest was "carried past here in
a box with three men on it, and two of them were weeping."

**What carries forward.** "Dropped." Verdite. The wood's alliance, which
matters in Act IV.

#### Ashen Moor
*Peat, standing water and burnt heather that never grew back.* Tier 3 to 4.
North.

**Arrival.** Heather burnt black across a whole horizon, three hundred years
ago, and never green since. A long hall of grey stone, roofed, lamplit,
absurdly intact in the desolation. A man in brown robes in the doorway, holding
a lamp up to see who is coming, smiling.

**The story here.** Ashen Moor is where the kingdom's memory lives and where
the story's betrayer is met, and the player should like him more than anyone
else on the map. Brother Hesk keeps the Grey Hall, the kingdom's library, alone,
and he can read the old script, and he has been waiting thirty years for
someone to bring him things to read. The moor burned the year of the Fall
because the Guest passed over it, or because the peat caught, or because the
kingdom burned it to stop something following the Guest; Hesk has all three
accounts and thinks the third is true. Tam's father came here. Hesk remembers
him. He went down the Ashen Steps and did not come up.

**People.** Brother Hesk. A hamlet of peat-cutters who bring him food and
think he is a saint. The Ashen Steps' cultists, who think the same and mean it
differently.

**The threads.**
1. The Grey Hall. Hesk reads everything you have found: the keys, the
   marks, the rhyme if you have it. He is delighted. He tells you what a weir
   is for, and what the seventh one is made of, and stops, and says "I would
   like to see the Stone Garden's tally before I say the rest."
2. Tam's father. Hesk has his Walker's cloak. He gave it to Hesk to keep the
   moor's wet off a book. Hesk gives it to Tam.
3. The Ashen Steps: "down, and then down again, and the second flight is wider
   than the first." Cultists, wraiths, and at the bottom the sixth weir-mark
   and the last Walker's bones, with a note in a script Hesk can read and the
   player cannot. He reads it aloud. It says the Sink's keeper is dying and
   someone must be sent, and it is addressed to Maud.
4. Hesk asks to come with you to the Stone Garden. He should be allowed.

**Underground.** The Ashen Steps, two levels, the second wider than the first,
because it was a hall before it was a stair. Cultists, wraiths, a Bone Knight,
and the keeper, *the Abbot Below*, a Cultist who founded the order in the
Steps forty years ago after reading one page of Hesk's library that Hesk does
not know is missing.

**Out in the open.** *The Heather-Fire*, a Wraith made of the burning that
never stopped, which walks the moor at night as a line of small flames and
sets nothing alight. *The Peat-Cutters' Bear*, a Stoneback Bear that has learned
the cutters' work-songs and hums them, which is how you know it is close.

**What carries forward.** Hesk, walking beside you. The last Walker's cloak on
Tam. The note to Maud.

#### The Stone Garden
*Standing rocks in rows nobody planted, and the Warden at the bottom of the
cut.* Tier 4. North east.

**Arrival.** Standing stones in rows across a mountainside, each one carved
with a tally, each one taller than a house. A cut in the mountain with a door
the size of a barn, and the door is open, and it was not built for people. Deep
inside, something the size of the door turns its head.

**The story here.** The Stone Garden is the kingdom's account book. Every stone
is a tally of one year: what the weirs held, what they cost, who kept them. The
Warden of the Cut is the first true boss of the game and the kingdom's greatest
work: a construct built to guard the tally, immune to poison, weak to energy,
and it has been guarding a stone nobody has read in three hundred years. That
stone is the last one, cut in Ossian's last year, and it is the one Hesk wants.
The Stone Garden is the end of Act II, and the moment the story turns: Hesk
reads the last tally and does not tell you what it says.

**People.** Nobody lives here. Hesk, if he came. Tam, if he came. The Warden.

**The threads.**
1. The rows. Walking them with Hesk is the zone's first half: he reads each
   tally as you pass and the kingdom rises and falls in numbers. The weirs
   held. The weirs cost more every year. Then a year with one word on it.
2. The Warden's Cut. The door. The fight.
3. The last tally, at the bottom, behind the Warden. Hesk reads it in silence.
   He tells you the six weirs were stone and water, and the seventh was a
   person, and the Vale's keeper died last winter, and the Wake is moving. He
   does not tell you the other half of the stone, which is Ossian's own
   account of what the Guest said to him, in the Guest's script.
4. That night, at camp, Hesk is gone. He has taken the rhyme, the note to Maud,
   and Tam's father's knife, and he has left a letter that says he is sorry and
   he thinks he can end it and to look after the boy. He has gone south.

**Underground.** The Warden's Cut, one vast level. Iron golems, wyverns, and
**the Warden of the Cut**, the game's first boss: 4000 health, a knockback, a
stun, poison-immune, energy-weak, and the door it guards is the size of a barn
because so is it.

**Out in the open.** *The Tally-Keeper*, an Iron Golem that walks the rows and
re-cuts the stones where weather has worn them, and will fight only if you
deface one. *The Roost*, a Wyvern pair on the highest stone that takes it in
turns to hunt.

**What carries forward.** The truth about the seventh weir. Hesk's absence.
Everything the player has learned, now pointing south.

### Act III: The Vigils

#### Cinderreach
*A coast of black glass and red rock, and the sea steams where it meets it.*
Tier 4 to 5. East.

**Arrival.** A coast of black glass, the sea steaming where it touches. A mine
yard that rings underfoot. A hall on a headland with no roof and every wall
fused to glass, and inside it, on a chair, a figure in a crown, quite still,
facing the sea. The camera comes round to its face. Its eyes are open and it
is looking at the camera.

**The story here.** Cinderreach is Ossian's hall and the end of the glass road.
The Ashen King is the story's second boss and its most important conversation.
He will talk before he fights, and what he says is the Guest's side of the
story, filtered through the one man who ever heard it: that the Guest was
dropped here by something that wanted rid of it, that it does not want to be
here either, that it dreams, and that when Ossian tried to make it into a weir
against death it did exactly what he asked, for him alone, and he has not died
since and cannot. He is the proof that the Wake cannot be ended by welcoming
it. Fern's promise is kept or broken here: tell him his daughter is being good,
and he weeps, and the fight is different.

**People.** The Cinder Cut's miners, who mine emberite and voidrock off a coast
that kills them by forty and are paid enough not to mind. Ossian.

**The threads.**
1. The Cinder Cut: emberite and voidrock, and voidrock is the fourth ore of the
   Guest, the one that is a piece of it, and it is heavy and cold and does not
   reflect light. The yard is black glass and rings.
2. The hall. The Ashen King on his chair. He speaks first. What he says depends
   on what you carry: the coldiron sword, the rhyme, Fern's promise.
3. The fight, if it comes to one, and it does: he cannot stop being what he is
   and he knows it and asks you to be quick.
4. What he leaves: the Guest's crown, a circlet of glass that lets whoever
   wears it hear the Night March keeping step, from anywhere. And a last thing
   about Hesk: "a monk came through. I told him the same. He thanked me."

**Underground.** The Ashen King's Hall, one level, a fused throne room and the
crypt beneath it where Ossian's court died sitting up. Vampire knights, bone
knights, wraiths, and **the Ashen King**: 3200 health, undead, weak to holy,
casts, and has a phylactery, which is the crown, and which the player can
choose to break or wear.

**Out in the open.** *The Glass-Walker*, a Wraith that leaves footprints of
melted glass and follows the tide line at dawn. *The Cinder Cut's Bell*, a
Manticore that has learned the shift bell means food.

**What carries forward.** The crown. "Dropped," confirmed by the man it was
dropped on. Hesk was here.

#### The Drowned Coast
*Half a kingdom under the tide, and its owner still doing his rounds.* Tier 4
to 5. North east.

**Arrival.** A drowned city under clear water, towers and streets visible from
above, fish moving down its avenues. Stone steps coming up out of the sea onto
a headland. Dusk. The water at the foot of the steps moves, and a knight in
full plate walks up them, water pouring off him, and behind him, in a line,
the drowned, and at the top of the steps he stops and looks at the sea and
turns around and walks back down.

**The story here.** The Drowned Coast is Ysolt's ending and the Drowned
Knight's. Sir Caradoc held the sea gate and drowned holding it and has walked
up the steps every night since to find the sea still there. The drowned of the
coast follow him because he is the last order left. He is the story's third
boss and the Vale's mirror: he has been a weir made of a person for three
hundred years, and he has no keeper's chair, only duty, and it has not been
enough to bring the sea down but it has been enough to keep him. Ysolt sails
you here because her brother is in the line and she has decided to bring him
home whatever it costs, and it costs the knight's respect, which he gives her.

**People.** Ysolt, at the tiller. Sir Caradoc. Ysolt's brother, *Aled*, a
Drowned in a salvager's coat, tenth in the line.

**The threads.**
1. Ysolt's boat across the sound from the Broken Strand. The drowned city under
   the keel. She does not look down.
2. The steps at dusk. The knight's round. He does not attack anyone who does not
   stand in the line's way. Standing in it is the challenge.
3. Aled. Ysolt has to be the one to do it. The player can hold the line back,
   or fight the knight, or ask the knight, who says: "take him. I did not ask
   him to come."
4. The knight, asked about the seventh weir, laughs for the first time in three
   centuries and says the Weir Kings never built a weir that did not have a man
   standing in it, and that the stone was only ever there to give the man
   something to lean on.

**Underground.** The Drowned Knight's Steps, one level going down into the
drowned gatehouse, air pockets and flooded halls, drowned and vampire knights
and **the Drowned Knight**: 2800 health, undead, holy-weak, coast-bound, and he
leeches life with every blow because the sea taught him to. He can be fought
without being killed: yield at a quarter health and he stops, and asks you to
tell the Vale's keeper that the gate held.

**Out in the open.** *The Bell Under the Water*, an event: at low water on the
first night, the drowned city's bell rings from under the sea and every
drowned on the coast turns toward it. *The Harbourmaster*, a Drowned in a chain
of office who checks every boat that lands and will let Ysolt's pass because
he remembers her brother.

**What carries forward.** Aled, home or not. "The stone was only ever there to
give the man something to lean on."

#### The Frostcrown
*Snow to the waterline, and the giants here do not care that you are cold too.*
Tier 4 to 5. South west.

**Arrival.** Snow to the sea's edge in a country that should be temperate. A
mine yard that has never thawed, breathing cold out of its mouths. A giant,
forty feet tall, sitting on a mountainside with its back to the camera, and
the camera comes round to show it is sitting on the mouth of a shaft, and the
snow around the shaft is melted, and the giant is shivering.

**The story here.** The Frostcrown is the Wake's cold, and the frost giants are
the people who have been paying for it. The Frostcrown Shaft "goes down until
it is warm, and that is worse": at its bottom is a vein of the Guest's own heat,
and the giants came here three centuries ago to sit on it, because a giant is
the only thing large enough to be a weir against warmth. Their thane, *Ulfra
Coldseat*, is the story's clearest ally among the Powers and its most tragic:
she knows exactly what she is doing and why, and her people are dying of it,
and she will let the player go down the Shaft if they can promise it will help,
and the player cannot honestly promise.

**People.** The frost giants, a dozen, on the mountain. Ulfra. The Rime Cut's
miners, who are giants too, mining rimesteel, the fifth ore of the Guest, the
one that grew where its heat met the giants' cold.

**The threads.**
1. The giants do not attack anyone who does not attack them. The zone's fear
   is being small.
2. Ulfra, on her shaft. She knows the Vale. "Your people sit on it too. Ours
   just sits harder." She has a message for Maud: "tell her we are still here."
3. The Rime Cut, rimesteel, and the smith among the giants who will work it for
   someone who brings word from the Iron Shoulder's Hild, whom she trained.
4. The Shaft. Ulfra moves. The way down is warm, and warmer, and at the bottom
   is a vein of something that glows and a hydra that came up out of it, and
   the wall at the bottom has the Guest's script on it, and someone has
   recently scraped the frost off to read it. Hesk.

**Underground.** The Frostcrown Shaft, three levels, cold then warm then hot,
dire wolves and frost giants who have gone wrong above, and at the bottom **a
Hydra** as the keeper, *Nine-Cold*, that grew in the vein and is the first thing
the player has fought that is made of the Guest.

**Out in the open.** *The Shivering Giant*, above, who is Ulfra. *The White
Pack*, a dire wolf pack that hunts the giants' herd and has a leader,
*Rimemouth*, with frost in his coat that does not melt.

**What carries forward.** Ulfra's message. Hesk's trail, going south. Proof
that the Guest can be held, and what it costs.

#### The Teeth
*Black rock standing up out of the ground in rows, and nothing between the
rows.* Tier 5. West.

**Arrival.** Black stone teeth in rows across a mountainside, and between them
nothing at all, no snow, no moss. A cave mouth with web across it, and the web
is thick as cable and it is holding the mouth up. The camera goes in along a
strand. Something the size of a house is sleeping at the centre of the web, and
under it, held in the web, is a piece of something black that is not stone.

**The story here.** The Teeth are the Mother of Spiders' and the story's one
Power that cannot be spoken to. Her web is the only thing on the continent the
Wake has never crossed, and she anchored it here three hundred years ago
around a piece of the Guest that broke off when it was carried past, and she
has been wrapping it since. She is not good. She eats what comes in. But she
is a weir, and the oldest thing in the Teeth's rows is her, and the treants of
the Long Dark call her sister. The Teeth are the game's hardest open zone and
the place the player understands the Wake can be *bound*, not ended, and that
everything that binds it becomes something else.

**People.** None. The Teeth have no people and never did. Voidrock and starfall
lie on the surface here, unmined, because nothing that came to mine them left.

**The threads.**
1. The rows. Walking between them, nothing lives. That is the whole first hour.
2. The Mother's Deep: web across the mouth, load bearing. Cutting it brings the
   roof down on the first chamber; the way in is along it.
3. The centre. The Mother, asleep. Under her, the shard. Take it and she wakes.
   Leave it and she does not, and the player can leave with what they have
   seen.
4. The shard, if taken, is the only piece of the Guest that can be carried into
   the Vale without the Wake following it, because it has been wrapped for
   three hundred years. It is what the Sink needs, in Act IV, to hold a little
   longer. Taking it costs the Teeth their weir.

**Underground.** The Mother's Deep, two levels of web and dark, giant spiders
without number and **the Mother of Spiders**: 2600 health, poison that stacks
to three, a web that roots for two seconds, and she is fought on her own web,
which is the floor, which she can move.

**Out in the open.** *The Rows' Silence*, an event: nothing. For the whole zone.
The player's own footsteps and nothing else, and it should be the most
frightening sound design in the game. *The Cyclops of the Teeth*, one Cyclops
that lives at the rows' edge and eats what the Mother does not, and is blind
in its one eye, and hunts by the silence breaking.

**What carries forward.** The shard, or the knowledge of it. Sister.

#### The Night March
*Dark trees on frozen ground, and the things in them keep step.* Tier 4 to 5.
North.

**Arrival.** Frozen forest at dusk. Silence, then a sound: a step, in unison,
from a thousand feet. Down a shaft in the frozen ground they come, in order, in
ranks, dead soldiers in the kingdom's colours, and they march up out of the
shaft and into the trees and stand in rows facing south, and do nothing, and
at dawn they march back down, in order.

**The story here.** The Night March is the Guest dreaming. The dead of the
kingdom's last army, who marched north to draw the Wake off and died in the
frozen wood, come up every dusk because something in the south is dreaming of
an army and they are the nearest one. They do not attack. They stand. Wearing
the Ashen King's crown, the player can hear them, and what they are saying, all
of them, quietly, in unison, is the word the Kiln-Warden said: open. The Night
March is the story's clearest warning about the third ending: wake the Guest
and this army marches where it is told.

**People.** None living. The Cold Adit's ice has a prospector in it, standing,
preserved, three hundred years dead with a pick raised. He is the zone's
loneliest sight.

**The threads.**
1. Dusk. The March. Stand in the rows and nothing happens. Stand in their way
   and everything does.
2. The Cold Adit: ice a hand thick, rimesteel and verdite, the prospector. His
   pack has a letter home in it, which is the last ordinary human voice in the
   game before the end.
3. The Night Shaft, by day, when the March is below: down, past the ranks,
   standing in the dark in their rows with their eyes open, to the bottom,
   where the shaft opens on a chamber with the Guest's script all over it and
   a general on a stone chair with his sword across his knees who is the
   March's keeper and has been waiting for orders.
4. The crown, worn here: the general looks up. "Are you it?" The player can say
   yes. This is the one place in the game the player can lie to the dead and
   the world remembers it.

**Underground.** The Night Shaft, two levels of ranked dead who do not fight
unless you break rank, and at the bottom **the General of the March**, a Lich
promoted with a name, *Marshal Corvin*, 520 health and every soldier in the
shaft if he calls them, and he only calls them if the player has said yes.

**Out in the open.** *The March*, above. *The Straggler*, a Bone Knight that
missed the last step down and has been trying to find the shaft by daylight
for three hundred years and asks directions.

**What carries forward.** "Open." The army that waits for a word. The general's
question.

#### The Sallow Wastes
*Nothing grows and something fell here, and the second fact explains the
first.* Tier 4 to 5. South.

**Arrival.** A desert that was a forest: petrified trunks standing in sand. A
crater a mile across with a mine yard on its rim, and the miners are not
mining the hill, they are mining what landed in it. At the crater's centre, a
shaft going down. On the rim, a man in brown robes with his back to the camera,
reading the crater floor like a page. He turns. It is Hesk's face and it is not
Hesk's eyes.

**The story here.** The Wastes are the Fall, and the end of Act III, and where
Hesk is found and lost. He has read the last tally, walked the glass road
backward to its start, gone down the Sallow Fall, and spoken to the Guest, and
the Guest has half woken, and the Wake is moving. Hesk is a lich now, still
kind, still patient, still explaining, and he does not think anything has gone
wrong. He thinks he needs one more thing to finish: a Weir. The last of the
line. He has asked the player to look after the boy, and he meant it, and he
also means to use him. The Sallow Fall is a dungeon that goes down into the
Guest itself, and its floor is a crater biome: cyclops, treants that grew from
nothing, hydras, and a bone dragon that was the kingdom's last dragon and came
here to die and did not.

**People.** The Sallow Fall's miners, who mine starfall, the last ore, the one
that is the Guest's own bone, and who are all cultists now, and were all
miners before, and do not see a difference. Hesk.

**The threads.**
1. The petrified forest. Every tree is facing away from the crater.
2. The rim. Hesk. He is glad to see you. He explains: the Guest is a child, in
   the way the treants are children of something, and it is having a
   nightmare, and the Wake is the nightmare leaking, and it can be woken, and
   he has almost done it, and a Weir's voice would finish it because the Guest
   remembers Ossian's. He asks where Tam is.
3. The Fall: down through the mine into the crater's throat, past the point
   where the walls stop being rock. The floor at the bottom is not a floor. The
   dragon is there. So is Hesk, at the end, and he does not fight you. He asks
   you to choose. The player can kill him, and he lets them, or leave him, and
   he waits.
4. Whatever is chosen, the Wake surges. The player feels it as the world
   changing on the way home: the map's danger bands step up one, everywhere,
   for the rest of the game. The Vale's hedges are down. Act IV.

**Underground.** The Sallow Fall, three levels: the mine, the throat, the
floor. Cultists and cyclops above, elder treants and hydras in the throat, and
on the floor **the Bone Dragon**, *Ashrend*, 1100 health as the roster has it,
promoted with a name and a phase where it stops fighting and speaks in Hesk's
voice, and **Brother Hesk**, who is the Lich of the roster with his own name
and his own face, and who is the only boss in the game who can be walked away
from.

**Out in the open.** *The Cultist Shift*, an event: at the change of shift the
miners walk up out of the Fall in a line and sit on the rim facing the centre
for an hour and go back down. *The One Who Left*, a Cyclops that was a miner
and grew, and walks the petrified forest looking for the road home to the
Ember Flats.

**What carries forward.** The Wake, moving. Hesk, dead or waiting. The choice
of the third ending, held open.

### Act IV: The Wake

#### The Bracken Sink
*Under the Vale.* Tier 5. Home.

**Arrival.** The Vale at night, hedges down, torches in the lanes, wolves and
worse in the wheat and the people of the Vale fighting them with what they
have. Maud at the door behind her cottage, and the door is open, and a wind is
coming up out of it. Tam beside her with his father's cloak and his father's
knife. She looks at you and says the thing she should have said at the start.

**The story here.** The Sink is the seventh weir, and it was never stone. The
first people of the Vale, fleeing Ossian's coast, found the one place on the
continent where the Wake pooled deepest and understood that it could only be
held by someone standing in it, and they built a stair down to the deepest
point and a chair at the bottom, and somebody has sat in it ever since. The
stair is a dungeon now because the chair is empty and the Wake has come up it:
the Sink is a mirror of every dungeon in the game, its levels the kingdom's
history in the wrong order, its dead the Vale's own keepers standing up out of
their rest to see who is coming. At the bottom, the chair. The choice.

**People.** Maud, at the mouth, who does not come down. Tam, who does. Every
named ally who is still alive and was earned: Ysolt at the mouth with a boat
crew, Hild with the Shoulder's miners, Ulfra's message, the Long Dark's
alliance as a treant standing in the Vale's lane with its roots in the hedge,
the Drowned Knight's word that the gate held. The Vale's own people, in the
lanes, doing what the Vale has always done.

**The threads.**
1. The lanes. Fighting through the Vale to Maud's door is the act's first
   movement, and every ally the player earned is in it somewhere.
2. Maud. What a keeper is. What her sister was. Why the stones said your name.
   "You can stand in it. Not for ever. Longer than us."
3. The stair. Down through levels that are the Vale's own dead keepers, forty
   of them across three centuries, each standing at the landing where they
   sat, none attacking anyone who says their name, which is cut into the wall
   beside them.
4. The chair. Tam says it should be him. The game's last choice. Or the third
   door, which is the way back up and out and south, to finish what Hesk
   started, with the crown and the shard and the general's army waiting for a
   word.

**Underground.** The Bracken Sink, five levels, one per act and one more, and
the keepers on the landings, and the Wake itself as the dungeon's weather:
darker with every level, and at the bottom, the boss is the chair, and there
is nothing to fight, and that is the hardest fight in the game.

**Out in the open.** The Vale at war. Every open-world encounter from Act I,
promoted: Old Grist leading the boar out of the beech hangar against the
wolves, because the Vale's own things fight for it too. The Sexton ringing the
bell all night.

**What carries forward.** Whoever sits. The credits. The world, changed to
match, for as long as the save lasts.

## 7. The ores are the story's spine

The ore ladder was built before this book, and it happens to be the Guest's
anatomy. This is worth writing down so the smiths' dialogue and the item
descriptions can say it.

| ore | what the story says it is | where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| copper, tin, iron | the world's own | everywhere the kingdom mined |
| silver | the kingdom's coin, and holy to the dead | the Barrows' offerings, the Shoulder |
| emberite | fell with the Guest, burns without fire | the Ember Flats, the glass road |
| coldiron | grew where the Wake's cold pooled | the Iron Shoulder, the Long Dark's edge |
| verdite | grew where the Wake touched living things | Marl's Seam, Witchwood's roots |
| rimesteel | the Guest's heat meeting the giants' cold | the Frostcrown, the Night March |
| voidrock | a piece of the Guest itself, heavy, dark | Cinderreach, the Teeth, the Fall |
| starfall | the Guest's bone | the Sallow Fall, and the Teeth's surface |

A weapon of the Guest's metals should say so when identified, and the smith who
makes it should ask where you mean to use it. Carrying starfall into the Vale
should be a thing Maud notices.

## 8. How the story is told without quests

The pillar stands: no quest giver, no log of tasks, no exclamation marks. The
story is delivered by five things the game already half has, and one it needs.

**1. Arrival.** The zone banner and its toast are the first line of every
chapter. The cinematic the user will make plays here, once, on first entry. The
`discovery.checkZone` event already fires exactly once per zone per character.
That is the hook.

**2. The world remembers what you have seen.** This is the one new system. A
`story.js` with a flat set of **facts** on the character record: `saw:weirmark`,
`met:tam`, `has:rhyme`, `promised:fern`, `killed:warden`, `read:tally`,
`hesk:gone`, `said_yes:corvin`, and so on. A fact is set by a trigger the
world already produces: entering a zone, standing near a site, killing a named
monster, picking up a named item, ending a conversation on a particular line.
Nothing asks the player to do anything. The facts are what they did.

**3. People say different things.** The talk window exists. Named NPCs get
lines keyed on facts: what Maud says depends on which act you are in; what
Ysolt says depends on whether you have been to Saltmere; what Hesk reads
depends on what you carry. A line is a `{ when: [facts], say: text, sets:
[facts] }` row. The conversation is the story. The same window that sells
bandages tells it.

**4. Things in the world are readable.** Stones, coffins, a letter in a pack, a
tally, the wall at the bottom of a shaft. `interact.js` already handles
standing near a thing and pressing E; a readable is a site-like record with a
text and a fact it sets. Hesk reads the ones the player cannot, which is why
losing him costs something: after Act II the player carries a script they
cannot read and has to find another reader, and there are two, and one is a
lich and the other is a dead king.

**5. Named encounters and events.** A semi-boss is a monster row with a
`name`, a `site` it stands at, an optional `habit` (a time, a trigger) and a
`says` line. `monsters.js` has the promotion mechanism in `boss()` already; the
open-world names are the same thing at smaller numbers. Events (the Rows at
Midnight, the Birds, the Bell Under the Water, the March) are timed spawns
with a script and no loot, and they are what make a zone feel inhabited by
something other than a spawn table.

**6. The journal.** Not a quest log. A book, on a codex tab, that writes itself
in the player's voice from the facts: "Saltmere. The Steps go down into the
sea and there is a door at the bottom. Ysolt would not say why the water is
where it is." One paragraph per fact that deserves one, in the order they were
learned. A player who has forgotten what they are doing reads their own book.
A player who has never read it loses nothing, because the world will say it
again.

**What the acts gate.** Nothing physical. The Sink's door is the one exception,
and it is a door in the world with a woman standing at it saying not yet. The
Powers of the rim can be walked to on day one and they will kill a tier 1
character, which the danger bands already do. What the acts gate is words: the
General does not ask his question of someone without the crown; Fern does not
sing to someone with a drawn sword; Hesk does not read the tally to someone who
has not brought him the Barrows' name. Knowledge is the key, and the keys are
things the player has to have done.

**Endings.** Three facts at the chair: `sat:you`, `sat:tam`, `sat:nobody`. The
world reads them. Danger bands step down or up. Named NPCs get their last
lines. A character who sat is retired to the chair and a new character can find
them there, which is the one piece of persistence this asks of the save that it
does not already have.

## 9. For the cinematics

Every zone's **Arrival** paragraph above is the shot list. Some notes that hold
across all of them:

- Every arrival ends on a **living thing looking back**: the boy on the roof,
  the drowned man on the steps, the golem with snow on its shoulders, the King's
  open eyes, the giant shivering, Hesk turning. The world is inhabited and it
  has noticed you.
- No narration. The zone's one line is the only text, in the banner's serif,
  and it appears as the shot ends.
- Weather and time of day are the zone's own: the Flats at noon, the Barrows
  at nightfall, Witchwood in falling blossom, the Night March at dusk, the Sink
  by torchlight.
- Music: the two themes rotate by biome now. Each act could take one: I is the
  Vale's, II the kingdom's, III the rim's, IV the Vale's again, slower.
- The Vale's arrival is the game's opening and should be the longest. The
  Sink's is the game's ending and should be the shortest: a door, a wind, a
  woman, a boy, and the line.

## 10. What this asks of the code, in order

1. `src/mmo/story.js`: facts, the named characters, their lines keyed on facts,
   the readables, the named encounters and events, and the three endings, as
   data. Node-testable. An `auditStory()` that proves every fact set is read
   somewhere and every fact read is set somewhere, so a thread can never dangle.
2. Named NPC placement: six people at fixed sites, with the talk window
   reading `story.js` before it reads the shop catalog.
3. Readables in `interact.js`, and the journal tab.
4. Semi-boss promotion in `monsters.js`: a name, a site, a habit, a line, on a
   rostered row. Events as timed spawns with no loot.
5. The Sink: sealed door, Act IV unseal, the keepers on the landings, the
   chair, the three endings, the retired character.
6. Zone-state: the Vale's four states, the world's danger step after the Fall,
   the rim's thaw after the third ending.

Each of these is a wiring note on its own. None of them moves a zone.
