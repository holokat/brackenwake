# Brackenwake: the ones you do not forget

`11-CAST.md` puts sixty named people in the world. Most of them are there so a
zone has voices in it. This book is about the thirteen who are there so the
player remembers the game ten years later, and it says what each of them is
built from, so the writing and the systems can be held to it.

## What makes a character impossible to forget

Not a backstory. Nobody remembers a backstory. Five things, and a character
in this book has all five:

1. **A contradiction you can say in one sentence.** A kind man who ends the
   world. A king who cannot die because he asked not to. A dead child keeping
   a stone clean for a father who killed the kingdom. The sentence is the
   hook; a player tells it to a friend.
2. **A signature.** One thing they always do or say, so that the second time
   you meet them you know them before they speak, and the last time it breaks
   your heart because it is the same. Hesk says "oh" softly. Caradoc has one
   message. Ulfra shivers and the snow melts.
3. **A secret that flips them.** Something the player learns, late, that makes
   every earlier scene mean something else. Not a twist for its own sake: the
   flip has to make them *more* themselves, not different.
4. **A mechanic.** Something the player *does* with them that nobody else in
   the game offers, so the memory is in the hands, not only the ears. You lie
   to Corvin. You light Lowri's lamps. You give the Straggler directions. You
   keep or break a promise to a dead girl and a king weeps or does not.
5. **They come back.** A character met once is a set piece. A character met
   four times across the map, changed each time, is a person. Everyone in
   this book is in at least two zones or two acts, and their last scene rhymes
   with their first.

And one rule over all five: **they want something the player cannot give them.**
Ossian wants to be asked. Fern wants to go home. Ysolt wants her brother. Ulfra
wants her people to stop dying. The player can help, or end it, or lie. They
cannot give it. That is what makes the player remember: the thing they could
not do.

## The thirteen

### 1. Maud Fallow, who sends you and lies about why

**Hook.** The old woman who reads the stones sent you out to save the valley
and knew the whole time that the valley is saved by someone sitting in a
chair until they die, and that her sister was that someone, and that you are
next.

**Signature.** She touches the nearest boundary stone before she answers
anything. In Act IV, at the Sink's mouth, there is no stone, and she reaches
for one, and her hand closes on nothing, and she tells you the truth.

**Secret.** She did not send you to find out why the hedges fail. She knows
why. She sent you out so you would be strong enough to come back and sit down,
and so she would not have to ask a stranger. She has been choosing between
you and Tam since the day the stones named you.

**Mechanic.** Every return to the Vale between acts, Maud has one more thing
to say that she should have said before, and the player can press her or let
her go. Pressing costs nothing in the world and everything in the scene: she
answers, and the answer is always half. The journal records what she has
admitted so far. By Act IV the player has a list of the things Maud did not
say, and she says them all.

**The scene.** The Sink's mouth. Torchlight, wind coming up the stair, the Vale
fighting behind you. "You can stand in it. Not for ever. Longer than us." And
then, because Tam is there: "Or he can."

**Recurs.** Every act, in the Vale. Her cottage is the door.

### 2. Tam Weir, who grows up while you watch

**Hook.** The miller's boy who follows you because he has nothing else to do
turns out to be the last of the line that built the kingdom, and the one
person the chair fits better than you.

**Signature.** He is always slightly ahead of you on the road. In Act IV, on
the stair down the Sink, he is behind you for the first time, and you notice.

**Secret.** He has known since the Weir Tower. Wynn told him not to go south,
and he heard why, and he never said, and he kept walking with you anyway. When
he says at the chair "it should be me," it is not a boy's impulse. He decided
two acts ago.

**Mechanic.** Tam levels. Not in numbers: in what he does. Act I he is a
slingshot and a warning. Act II, taught the bow by Brannoc if the player let
him, he shoots. Act III he carries his father's cloak and knife and stands in
fights. He can die, in the world, at any point, and if he does the game goes
on without him and the chair has one fewer candidate and Maud's last line is
different. Keeping Tam alive to the end is the game's one escort quest, and it
is never written down anywhere.

**The scene.** Thornwood, the cold fire, his father's knife in his hand. He does
not cry. "Can I keep walking with you?"

**Recurs.** Every zone the player takes him to. Nearly all of them.

### 3. Brother Hesk, the kindest man on the continent, who ends it

**Hook.** The gentle scholar who is the only person able to read the old
words reads them, believes them, and goes to wake the thing everyone else has
spent three hundred years keeping asleep, because he thinks it is a child
having a nightmare and someone should hold it.

**Signature.** He holds every object you show him up to the lamp, turns it,
and says "oh" softly before he says anything else. In the Sallow Wastes, as a
lich, you show him the shard, and he holds it up to a light that is not
there, and says "oh."

**Secret.** He is right. Or right enough that the game never says he is wrong.
The Guest was dropped, does not want to be here, dreams badly, and the third
ending proves that waking it stops the Wake. Hesk's crime is not being wrong.
It is doing it alone, and needing a Weir's voice to finish, and knowing whose.

**Mechanic.** Hesk is the reader. Every readable in the world the player cannot
read, Hesk can, and the player carries them to him and he reads them aloud,
and what he reads is the story. When he leaves, the player is illiterate in
their own story until they find another reader, and there are two, and one is a
dead king and the other is Hesk. The player *feels* the betrayal as a lost
ability.

**The scene.** The camp below the Stone Garden, morning, Hesk's blanket folded,
a letter on it. "I think I can end it. Look after the boy." He has taken Tam's
father's knife. The player realises, later, why.

**Recurs.** Ashen Moor, the Stone Garden, the Sallow Wastes, and his voice in
the Bone Dragon's mouth.

### 4. Ysolt of the Strand, who will not sail east until she will

**Hook.** The salvager who has spent twenty years charting a drowned coast she
refuses to sail to, because her brother is standing on its steps every night
in a line of the dead, and she has not decided what she owes him.

**Signature.** She sews, and never pricks herself, and never looks at the
needle. On the boat to the Drowned Coast she is not sewing. Her hands are on
the tiller and they are shaking and she does not hide it.

**Secret.** She has been to the Drowned Coast. Once. She saw Aled in the line
and rowed back and told nobody, including Hedd, and the "two boats lost" were
one boat lost and one boat she scuttled so she would have an excuse not to go
again.

**Mechanic.** Ysolt is the boat. The Drowned Coast cannot be reached without
her, and she will not sail for money, only for a reason she believes, and the
reasons are: the player has met the Ashen King, or the Frostcrown's thane, or
carries the coldiron sword. Then, at the Steps, Aled is her choice and not the
player's: the player can hold the line, or fight the knight, or ask him, but
the hand that touches Aled is Ysolt's, and the player watches.

**The scene.** Aled stops in the line. The line stops behind him. Caradoc turns
his head. Ysolt walks down three steps into the water in her boots and puts a
hand on her brother's face, and the knight says: "Take him. I did not ask him
to come."

**Recurs.** Saltmere, the Broken Strand, the Drowned Coast, the Vale in Act IV
with a boat crew who came inland for the first time in their lives.

### 5. Fern, who is ten and has been ten for three hundred years

**Hook.** A dead child keeps the moss off a stone in a silent wood, waiting for
a servant to come and take her home to her father, who was the king who ended
the kingdom, and she does not know that she is dead or that he is on a chair of
glass or that the blossom falling around her is the wood grieving for her.

**Signature.** She brushes petals off the stone as she talks, and there are
always more. When the player comes back from Cinderreach with an answer for
her, the petals have stopped falling, for the length of the scene.

**Secret.** She knows. Not in words. She knows the way children know a thing
adults have decided not to tell them: she asks if her father has stopped being
angry because she remembers him weeping as he put her on the horse, and she
has spent three hundred years deciding that meant anger, because the other
thing it could mean is worse.

**Mechanic.** The promise. She asks you to tell her father she is being good.
You can promise. At Cinderreach, before the Ashen King, the game gives you the
line, and you can say it or not, and the fight is different either way: he
weeps and does not use the crown and is slow, or he does not and it is what it
is. Then you come back to Witchwood, and she asks what he said, and you tell
her the truth or you do not, and the wood answers: the blossom falls all at
once, or the birds come back. Nobody tells the player this is a mechanic. It
is just a promise.

**The scene.** "Has my father stopped being angry?"

**Recurs.** Witchwood twice, and her name on Anwen Tide's family tree in
Cinderreach with no date of death, and Sir Bryn in the Long Dark: "I left her
at a stone in a wood where the blossom was. I did not go back."

### 6. Ossian, the Ashen King, who asked not to die

**Hook.** The last king welcomed the thing that fell, asked it for a weir
against death, and got one, for himself alone, and has sat on a chair of glass
for three hundred years being the proof that the Wake cannot be ended by
loving it.

**Signature.** He does not move anything but his eyes until he stands, and
when he stands the glass floor cracks under him. Before that: "Sit down.
Nobody has sat down in a very long time." There is a second chair. Sitting in
it is the game's most frightening button.

**Secret.** He never wanted the throne. He was the youngest, and his brothers
died in the flood year, and the weir against death was not for him. It was
for Fern. He asked it to keep his daughter alive, and it heard "keep the one
who asks," and she died on the road inland anyway, and he has known for three
hundred years that the kingdom drowned for a wish that missed.

**Mechanic.** The conversation is the fight's difficulty. Every true thing the
player brings him (the rhyme, the sword, Bryn's account of him weeping, Fern's
promise) is a line he answers, and each answer changes the fight: slower, no
crown, no crypt summon, until, with everything, he stands and says "be quick"
and the fight is a formality and the player has to do it anyway. A player who
brings nothing fights the full boss. Both are the right ending. Only one is
remembered.

**The scene.** The promise kept. "She is being good." He puts a hand over his
eyes. Glass runs down his face, because it is glass.

**Recurs.** Named in the Barrows (an empty coffin), the Hollow Hills (Wynn's
recitation), the Long Dark (Bryn saw him weep), Cinderreach, and the crown he
leaves, which speaks in the Night March.

### 7. Sir Caradoc, the Drowned Knight, who has one message

**Hook.** The knight marshal who held the sea gate until the water was over his
head and has walked up the same steps every night for three hundred years to
find the sea still there, carrying one message for a keeper he has never met,
with nobody to give it to.

**Signature.** Water pours off him the whole time he speaks and the pool at
his feet never grows.

**Secret.** He knows the gate did not hold. The sea came over it. He held his
post while it did, and the message he has carried for three centuries is a
lie he has told himself so long it has become a duty, and when the player
brings him the truth about the seventh weir he understands, at last, what he
has been: not a failed gate. A weir with a man in it. He laughs, once. It is
the saddest sound in the game.

**Mechanic.** He can be fought without being killed. Yield at a quarter health
and he stops and gives you the message. Kill him and the drowned scatter and
the coast is safer and the message is lost, and Maud in Act IV asks if you met
him, and you say what you did. Either is allowed. The game never says which
was right.

**The scene.** "The Weir Kings never built a weir that did not have a man in
it. The stone was only ever there to give the man something to lean on." And
then: "Tell the Vale's keeper the gate held."

**Recurs.** The Drowned Coast, and the Reeve at Saltmere is his, and his line
is what Maud repeats at the chair.

### 8. Ulfra Coldseat, forty feet of woman shivering on a hole in the world

**Hook.** The thane of the frost giants sits on the mouth of a shaft that goes
down to the Guest's own heat, because a giant is the only thing large enough
to be a weir against warmth, and her people have been dying of the cold she
makes for three hundred years, and she knows, and she sits.

**Signature.** She shivers. Every time she does, the snow around the shaft
melts a little further, and she looks at it.

**Secret.** She and Maud have written to each other, by runner, every ten years,
for fifty years. Two women sitting on two holes. Neither has ever seen the
other. Ulfra's message for Maud is "tell her we are still here," and Maud's
last message to Ulfra, which the player never sees, said "my sister is dying,
I do not know who is next," and Ulfra has been waiting for the answer for a
year. The player is the answer.

**Mechanic.** The giants do not attack. The whole zone is being small among
people who could end you and choose not to, and the fear is different from
every other zone's fear. Ulfra moves off the shaft once, for the player, if
they carry a message for Maud, and while she is off it the zone gets warmer,
visibly, the snow line creeping down while you are underground, and you come
up and she is back on it and it is snowing again.

**The scene.** "Your people sit on it too. Ours just sits harder."

**Recurs.** The Frostcrown, and Hild's apprentice is her daughter, and in Act IV
she cannot come to the Vale and sends the cold: the Sink's stair is frosted
from the third landing down, and Maud says "Ulfra," and that is all.

### 9. Marshal Corvin, who asks if you are it

**Hook.** A general who marched the kingdom's last army north to draw the Wake
off and drew it off and died in a frozen wood, and has sat at the bottom of a
shaft with his sword across his knees for three hundred years waiting for
someone to come and say the word, and does not know what the word will do.

**Signature.** His hand rests on the sword and never closes on it. If you say
yes, it closes.

**Secret.** He knows what the word will do. He has heard his men say it in
their sleep for three centuries. He asks "are you it?" not because he cannot
tell, but because he wants to be told no, and be allowed to keep waiting, and
he has never been allowed to want anything.

**Mechanic.** The lie. Wearing the Ashen King's crown, you can say yes. Nothing
happens. The March comes up at dusk as always. Nothing happens for the rest of
Act III. Then, in Act IV, if you take the third door and go south to wake the
Guest, the March is there, in ranks, in the Sallow Wastes, having walked the
length of the continent in the dark to stand behind you, because you said
yes. It is the only time the game rewards a lie, and it is the most
frightening reward in it. Say no and he says "then we wait," and they do, and
that is its own ending for him.

**The scene.** "Nobody came to tell us we could stop. Are you it?"

**Recurs.** The Night March, and the March itself anywhere the crown is worn,
and the Sallow Wastes in the third ending.

### 10. Lowri Lamp, who has not had a night off in thirty years

**Hook.** A woman who lights lamps along a tree line every dusk because the
dead stop at the lamps and she does not know why, and has done it every night
for thirty years, and her husband is in the third rank, and she has never told
anyone.

**Signature.** She trims a wick while she talks and her hands never stop. On
her night off, her hands are in her lap, and she does not know what to do with
them.

**Secret.** The dead do not stop at the lamps. They stop at her. She has been
standing in the Wake every dusk for thirty years, and she is a weir, a small
one, and does not know it, and the player, who has by now heard what a keeper
is, is the only one who can see what she is. The lamps are what she leans on.

**Mechanic.** The night off. Light the lamps for her, one dusk, all forty,
before the March comes up, and she sleeps for the first time in thirty years,
and in the morning she tells you about her husband, and the March that night
came to the lamps and stopped, as always, because the player was standing
there. The player feels their own weir-ness for the first time and the game
does not say so.

**The scene.** Morning. Her hands in her lap. "He is in the third rank. Fourth
from the left. He was tall. He still is."

**Recurs.** The Night March, and she is in the Vale's lane in Act IV with a lamp,
because she heard, and she says "one night off. You gave me one. I owe you the
rest."

### 11. Enid Brick, who bricked her husband in

**Hook.** A potter who walled her husband into a kiln from the outside when he
came back from the cellars changed, because she could not kill him and could
not let him out, and has thrown the same pot on the wheel every day since,
selling them to the bandits who live where he went wrong.

**Signature.** The pot. It is always the same pot. Nobody has ever seen her
finish one.

**Secret.** He knocks. Every night. She has sat with her back to the kiln every
night for eleven years listening to him knock, and the reason she will not let
the player open it in Act I is not fear. It is that she is not finished
listening.

**Mechanic.** The kiln is a door the player can open at any time. Enid asks
them not to, and the game lets them anyway, and what comes out is the Bricked
Man, a zombie in a potter's apron, and he is slow and sad and easy to kill and
the player should feel like a thief. Or the player waits, and in Act III she
asks, and the fight is the same and the feeling is not. Or the player never
does, and in Act IV she is in the Vale's lane with a pot, finished, the first
one, and gives it to you, and it is a funeral urn.

**The scene.** Act III. "You want to know why. Come back when you have been
down the Cellars." You have. She tells you about the knocking.

**Recurs.** Kiln Heath twice, the Vale in Act IV.

### 12. Aldous the Quiet, who corrects his skeleton's posture

**Hook.** A necromancer who has lived among a thousand graves for thirty years
asking the dead questions, not to use them or free them or heal them, but to
know, and who has learned more about the Wake than anyone alive and has told
almost none of it to anyone, because nobody asked properly.

**Signature.** A skeleton holds his lamp. He corrects its posture twice in
every conversation, gently, the way you would a nephew.

**Secret.** The skeleton is his brother. Aldous raised him the year he came to
the Barrows, and has never let him lie down again, and has never once asked
him a question, because he is afraid of the answer, and every other dead
thing on the continent has been a way of not asking.

**Mechanic.** Aldous teaches necromancy, and the first lesson is the game's
first raised skeleton, and the player's own skeleton behaves like his: it
holds things, it follows, it can be corrected. Necromancers who learn from
Aldous get a skeleton that is a little more person than the ones from the
book. And the Barrows' dead stand up at midnight and face the Fall, and Aldous
has never once stood with them, and if the player does, the next dawn he asks
what it was like.

**The scene.** Late, the Long Barrow, Act III, the player back from the rim.
Aldous asks what the King said. The player tells him. Aldous turns to the
skeleton with the lamp and says, for the first time, "Owain. Was it like that?"
And the skeleton does not answer, because they do not, and Aldous nods as if
it had.

**Recurs.** The Grey Barrows in every act, and the Sink's door in Act IV, with
his skeleton holding the lamp, and he goes down the stair behind you and stops
at the first landing and reads the name on the wall and stays there.

### 13. Selwyn, the Walker before, who offers cold water

**Hook.** The charming cultist captain who keeps turning up one zone ahead of
you with a cup of cold water in a hot country was the Vale's Walker two
generations ago, sent out by a younger Maud, and he found out what the hedges
are made of, and chose the Wake, and has been waiting for the next Walker to
explain it to.

**Signature.** He offers you water first, always, and it is always cold, and
nothing else around him is. In the Vale, in Act IV, in the lane, he offers
you water, and it is warm, and he looks at the cup, and it is the only time
he is not smiling.

**Secret.** Osric knows. "Every Walker orders the same thing." Selwyn sat in
the Bracken Arms' chair forty years ago and ordered it, and Osric, who was a
boy pulling pints, remembers, and has never told Maud he saw the same man in
Crookwell market twenty years later wearing the Guest's mark. Selwyn is what
the Vale does not talk about: the Walker who came back and was not let in.

**Mechanic.** The mark. Selwyn offers it at every meeting, and it is a real
item, and taking it sets a fact that every character in the game reads: the
cultists become friendly and everyone else notices, Maud most of all, and the
third ending becomes easier and the first two become harder, and it can be
taken off, and everyone remembers that you wore it. He is the game's one
recurring villain who is not a monster and never draws a weapon. You cannot
fight him. He walks away every time. In Act IV he is in the lane, and does not
walk away, and does not fight, and says: "I sat in that chair for a week. Did
she tell you that? A week. Then I got up. Ask her what she did."

**The scene.** Kiln Heath, the first meeting, before the player knows anything.
A man in clean robes in a filthy country, a cup of water so cold it hurts.
"You are the new one. She has not told you yet. She never does at first. Drink.
It is only water."

**Recurs.** Kiln Heath, the Ember Flats, the Sallow Wastes, the Vale in Act IV.
Always one zone ahead. Always the same cup.

## What this changes in the other two books

- `11-CAST.md`, the Recruiter: he is Selwyn, and he was a Walker, and his
  entry is rewritten below this line in that file.
- `11-CAST.md`, Osric: gains the topic "the Walker who came back" [met:selwyn].
- `11-CAST.md`, Aldous: the skeleton is Owain, his brother, and the Act III
  scene is his.
- `10-STORY.md`, Cinderreach: Ossian's wish was for Fern. The Arrival and the
  fight stand; the conversation gains the line.
- `10-STORY.md`, the Night March: the third-ending payoff of Corvin's yes is
  the March in the Sallow Wastes, and belongs in the Sallow Fall's Act IV
  description.
- `10-STORY.md`, the Sink: Lowri, Enid, Ysolt's crew, Aldous and Selwyn are in
  the lane, each with the line above.

The rule for anyone adding a character after this: if you cannot fill in all
five things in section one, they are a vendor. That is fine. Give them a tell
and a fact and let them sell bread. But do not put them in this book.
