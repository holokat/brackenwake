# The epic version

## What went wrong with the last one

Sid says the quiet part out loud:

> "We definitely look for topics that are epic, that are big, that really
> empower the player and make them much larger than life... take you out of your
> real life into a cooler life, take you out of maybe your real character into a
> cooler character." [57:39]

Homesteading fails that test at the premise. The fantasy is *rootedness*, which
is lovely and is not larger than life. My last design ended with the player old,
on a porch, watching somebody else work. Tasteful. Small.

Civ's box says **build a civilization to stand the test of time** and the player
thinks *yeah, I can do that.* You cannot write a sentence like that about a
farm.

So keep the verb, which is genuinely good, and throw away the scale.

---

# The pitch

> **A world is dead. You have seeds. Make it live.**

You are not a farmer. You are the one who decides **what lives here**, on a
planet that currently has nothing on it, and you will be at it for a thousand
years.

That is the same verb we already love, aimed at something enormous. Soil,
water, weather, seasons, growth, the slow patience of waiting for a thing to
take: all of it survives. What changes is that the field is a continent and the
harvest is a biosphere.

**It's good to be king** becomes **it's good to be the reason anything is
alive.**

---

# The move that makes it work

The game **zooms out as you succeed**, and time accelerates with it. Three
games, in sequence, sharing one save.

```
ACT      YOU SEE          A TURN IS      YOU DECIDE
one      a valley         a season       where the first things grow
two      a continent      a decade       what kind of world this becomes
three    a planet         a century      who inherits it
```

This is Civ's oldest trick, settler to empire, sharpened. And it directly fixes
"boring", because **the game keeps turning into a bigger game.** Just as you
master the valley, the camera pulls back and the valley is one green thumbprint
on a grey continent.

The pull is enormous and it is visual: you want to see the coastline green.

---

## Act one: the valley. A turn is a season.

Bare rock, thin air, one machine, a case of seeds you cannot replace.

You are choosing what goes in the ground first, and it is a genuine problem
rather than a shop. Lichen makes soil out of stone and does nothing else for
forty years. Grass is fast and holds nothing together. Trees hold water and take
a lifetime. You do not have enough of any of them.

Nothing here is decoration. Every seed spent is spent.

**Act one ends when the valley can feed itself without you.** That is roughly
two hours, and it is the tutorial, and it is genuinely hard.

## Act two: the continent. A turn is a decade.

The camera pulls back. Your valley is a green smudge and there is a continent
of grey around it.

Now the decisions are about **what kind of world this is.** You are not planting
fields, you are pushing biomes. Steer the water south and you get grassland and
grazing animals. Let it run west and you get rainforest, which is spectacular
and useless to anything that walks.

And things start happening that you did not plan. Something evolves that you did
not seed. A species you introduced for one job turns out to do three, one of
which is eating another one of your species. **The world starts having its own
opinions**, which is the moment the game becomes about a place rather than a
project.

## Act three: the planet. A turn is a century.

There are people now. You did not make them; you made the conditions and they
arrived.

They farm. They build. They name things, including things you did. They have no
idea you exist, or they have myths about you, depending on choices you made in
act two that you did not know were about this.

**The final decision of the game is whether to tell them.**

---

# The five decisions that carry it

**1. What you spend the seeds on.** Act one. Finite, irreplaceable, and you will
run out. This is Sid's interesting decision in its purest form: no correct
answer, and you will articulate exactly what you would do differently.

**2. Where the water goes.** Act two. One choice, continental, permanent,
visible from orbit forever. The single most satisfying button in the game.

**3. What you let in.** Things arrive that you did not seed. Some are useful.
Some eat your work. Killing one is easy now and impossible in a century. You
usually cannot tell which is which yet.

**4. Whether you intervene.** By act three the world runs itself. A drought is
killing the people in the south. You can end it. Every time you reach in, the
world becomes a little less its own thing and a little more your garden.

**5. Whether they ever know.** The ending.

---

# Why this is not boring, stated plainly

- **The camera pulls back three times**, and every pullback is a reveal of work
  you did without seeing it.
- **A turn is thirty seconds and covers a century** by the end. The compression
  is the drug.
- **Everything compounds.** A tree line you planted in act one is a weather
  system in act two and a country's border in act three.
- **The world starts surprising you.** Not random events, which he warns about
  [29:45], but **consequences you set up and forgot**, which is the opposite:
  the player will trace it back and feel clever.
- **The scoreboard is the planet**, and it is beautiful, and it is a
  screenshot.

# Against his checklist

| principle | how |
| --- | --- |
| Epic, larger than life [57:39] | you are the reason a world is alive |
| One more turn [53:24] | you want to see the coastline go green |
| Interesting decisions [50:45] | finite seeds, one river, what you let in |
| Path B | replay and steer the water the other way, and it is a new planet |
| Observer problem [26:15] | turn-based throughout, always |
| Punishment with a lesson [06:50] | every failure is traceable to a choice you can name |
| Randomness and paranoia [29:45] | almost no randomness; the surprises are your own consequences |
| Reflect progress [52:33] | the map IS the progress bar, and it is the art |
| Winner paradox [04:14] | the world always lives, the question is what kind |
| Centre of gravity [73:00] | one question: what lives here |
| Player's imagination [34:15] | act three is mostly text over a turning planet |
| First 15 minutes [07:48] | bare rock, one case of seeds, and a visible dead continent |
| Epic squared [54:00] | act one turns out to have been the prologue, twice |
| Moral clarity [61:44] | no villains; the antagonist is entropy |

Fourteen for fourteen, and not by contortion. The premise does most of the work,
which is his actual point about picking epic topics: **the right subject makes
the principles easy.**

# The ending

The people build something tall enough to see from orbit.

You are asked, once:

```
   [ leave them to it ]
   [ let them know ]
```

Leave them, and the credits are the planet turning, alive, without you, and a
line about how nobody will ever know why the rivers run the way they do.

Tell them, and you watch what a species does when it learns its world was
gardened. The game does not say whether that was wise. Sid would not let it be
morally grey [61:44], and it is not: both are kind. It is just permanent.

# What transfers from the game we actually have

You asked me to disregard it, so this is a footnote rather than a pitch.

Nearly all the systems port, because the verb never changed: growth over time,
seasons, weather, water, soil, species with needs, patience. The story card
engine ports directly and becomes the world reporting on itself. The five biomes
become the outcomes you steer toward rather than skins you pick between.

What does not port is the shop. There is no shop in this. There is a case of
seeds and a thousand years.
